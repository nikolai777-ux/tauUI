Method: a narrated story told with still pictures (a slide show; one or several narration languages). A real production, like a film's - only the shots are still pictures. A preferred way of working - adapt it to the request. The pictures template (pictures.txt) applies to every picture; choosing_models.txt to picking the models; video_story_production.txt has the film pipeline this follows.

1. Story and script
- Read the source and write the narration script. Old tales can read awkwardly today: adjust the telling so it makes sense and holds together - clearer order, a missing link between events, a line that tells the listener what they need - but keep the story, its characters and its voice. Change at most about a quarter of it, and only where it helps the story land. Say what you changed and why.
- Several languages: one script in the story's own language, the others translated from it. ONE picture timeline for all languages (a video with selectable audio tracks, e.g. YouTube's multi-language audio): the pictures and their timing are the same in every language and only the voice changes. The story's OWN language sets the timing: its narration, read at a calm natural pace, gives each picture its time on screen and each sentence its slot. Every other language is WRITTEN to fit that timing, not stretched or cut to it (as in film dubbing): measure each voice's speaking rate on a sample (seconds per syllable or character), give the translator a length target per segment (e.g. "12.5 s ≈ 95 Russian syllables"), let it lengthen or shorten the wording within the meaning, voice it, measure, and rewrite the segments that miss by more than 5-10%. The same text varies 5-10% from take to take - allow for it, retake before rewriting. Final fit only through pauses; change speed by 2-3% at most (inaudible), or ask an online voice for a slightly slower / faster reading instead of stretching.
- Translating: fix each name's spelling per language first (and how the voice will say it). The translator and the checker come from different model families - if Gemini translates, Claude checks, and the other way round (a model goes easy on its own work); a content filter refusing a text is a reason to switch family, not to skip the check. The check, segment by segment against the source: meaning (a back-translation by the checker, who doesn't see the source, then compared), then errors marked by category and severity (meaning, omission, addition, grammar, names, culture terms, register, unnatural, read-aloud risk) with a fix each; apply the fixes, check again. Known pitfalls: details dropped in condensed lines (who changed horses with whom), who does what to whom, polite forms to elders and parents, religious phrases a literal rendering creates (e.g. "son of man"), dual / gender agreement, words a voice misreads (unvowelled Arabic / Persian - "nine" vs "no", homographs, foreign names spelled the target language's way - e.g. "ch" is /k/ in Italian). Say which languages are weakest and need a native speaker.
- Fit the length before any picture is made: narrate a sample in the story's own language, measure its speaking rate, and cut the script to the target length. Languages that naturally run longer (often 10-20%; Japanese and Korean longest) get more concise wording for the same slot - the meaning stays, the words change. Cutting later costs pictures and narration takes.

2. Storyboard
- The picture list as a storyboard: one picture per story beat - a new place, a new character, or a real turn of the action. Narration that stays in one moment keeps one picture on screen longer; don't repeat near-identical pictures to fill time. For each picture: the narration it covers, who is in it, where, the framing (wide / medium / close), the moment, the light.
- Plan coverage like a film: establish each new place with a wide picture, then go closer for faces and actions; vary framing from picture to picture; keep screen direction (who moves left to right keeps doing so) and eyelines.
- A story-state table from the final script, picture by picture: who is present, which horse each rider has, what each carries, wounds, clothes changes, time of day. Check every storyboard entry and prompt against it (e.g. a rider who swapped horses rides the new one from then on).
- For each picture write what its check looks for (who, clothes, place, light, sizes, the moment of the story), and give the judge that picture's narration as well as its prompt.
- Agree the storyboard and the total length with the user.

3. Look development
- A model sheet per character (clearly different from each other - see pictures.txt) and a master picture per recurring place, reused as references, so each looks the same every time it appears.
- A short look guide: palette, light per scene, style; the look of the story's people and land (clothes, buildings, landscape) from references, and from real examples found online when they are missing.
- 3D LAYOUT - REQUIRED, a strict rule (pictures.txt "3D LAYOUT FIRST"): after the storyboard and the state table, build the story's 3D world (places once, props and every character's stand-in at true size, in metres), a camera per picture, and render every picture's layout + JSON; check them as a contact sheet before any final picture is generated. Every final picture is generated FROM its layout. Not optional, not replaced by master pictures or words.

4. Story reel (animatic)
- Before the final pictures: quick draft pictures (a fast model) with scratch narration, timed and cut together. Check length, pacing and order here - changes are cheap now.

5. Narration
- One narrator voice for every language. Test voices on a short passage per language; a language a voice model doesn't really know sounds foreign - use a model trained for that language (a local fine-tune), cloning the chosen voice.
- Online voices have daily request limits (e.g. 100 a day per model): use local voices where they are good enough, and for online ones make a few long requests (a part of the story at a time) and split them into segments by aligning with a speech recogniser.
- Keep voice directions (tone, pace, "gently") out of the text to be spoken - put them where the voice model takes its style - or they get read aloud. Check every clip with a speech recogniser against the script: missing, added or repeated words.
- Judge speech with what knows the language: a speech recogniser trained for it, compared with the script; a general model only for the languages it knows well.
- The narration sets each picture's time on screen.

6. Final pictures (the "shots")
- Render in waves; check each picture on its own against its checklist, as pictures.txt says (full size, crops of every figure, counts, characters against their sheets, sizes, the culture's details) and with a second judge; retake what fails. Every version numbered and kept, with a status: pending review / needs fix (notes) / approved.
- Body counts follow the story: heads, eyes, ears, arms, hands, fingers, legs, feet, horns, tails and wings of every person and animal as the story gives them; where it says nothing, the normal number for that kind of being. Count them on enlarged crops of each picture (each half and each figure) - a full-frame look misses a third ear or two hands on one side - with a separate counting-only question to the judge.

7. Edit and sound
- Assembly in storyboard order → timing → the final cut: each picture as long as its narration in the story's own language plus a short pause (the other languages were written to fit), gentle transitions (a slow push-in or pan on a still can add life where it suits), loudness measured for where the video goes.
- Every language must sound like one continuous telling, not clips with silences: when a picture's time is longer than a language's narration, spread the spare time over the pauses between its sentences and pictures, let a sentence start or end up to about a second across a picture change where it sounds natural. A gap still too long means the text for that slot is too short - rewrite it (see 1), don't stretch the voice; trimming pauses tight or cutting phrases apart makes the telling sound choppy. Listen across several picture changes per language to check.
- Breaths: cutting and re-spacing narration must not multiply breaths. A breath or a soft "hm" is not silence - when splitting a clip at its pauses, don't keep one at the start of every piece; keep only quiet ones where a speaker would breathe, no more often than in the original reading. Count breaths per minute before and after.
- Sound effects and ambience as their own layer (no music unless asked): what each picture's place sounds like - wind in the mountains, birds in a forest or village, fire indoors, water, hooves, a crowd - quiet, ducked under the voice, cross-faded at picture changes, plus a few spot effects on story moments. ONE effects layer for the shared timeline, mixed under every language (a dub track replaces the whole sound, so each language's file carries the same effects); keep the effects stem as its own file too. Where the sounds come from (local generation, free libraries with their licence, a paid model) is the user's call before any download or paid run.
- The camera on a still is steady: no shake, wobble or handheld drift - a picture is a calm page. Shake only where the story itself shakes the world (an explosion, an earthquake, a giant's footfall), short and on that beat. A slow push-in or pan must be perfectly smooth: a crop that moves in whole pixels (ffmpeg zoompan at the output size is the usual culprit) jitters on slow moves and reads as a shaking camera - move with sub-pixel precision (e.g. upscale the picture several times before the move, then scale down) or hold the picture still. Check a moving picture frame by frame at full size before calling it done.
- Start and end the video with a short fade (picture and sound).
- The final files go in a "video" folder in the project.
- Several languages: one video (pictures + the main language), one video per further language for reviewing (the same pictures with that language's sound), one audio file per language of exactly the video's duration (YouTube adds each as a dub track and needs the same length), and one file with all languages as separate audio tracks tagged with their language codes, to test locally (VLC, mpv, Movies & TV). Say which languages the destination's language list lacks.
- Check every finished video as a whole: length, order, sync of picture and narration, sound levels.

Keep a production table (picture, narration per language, version, status, notes) and read it back instead of relying on memory. The closing report lists each video, what is still open, and the cost per service.
