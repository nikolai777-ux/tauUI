Method: making pictures (portraits, characters, places, props, frames for video). A preferred way of working - adapt it to the request, EXCEPT the 3D layout rule below, which is not adaptable.

3D LAYOUT FIRST - A STRICT RULE (the user, 2026-10-08; not a suggestion, never skipped or swapped for words or master pictures): every picture that shows a place, more than one figure, or a figure next to a building, animal or prop is laid out in a 3D model of its world BEFORE it is generated, and generated FROM that layout. Only a single portrait or a reference sheet on plain paper is exempt.
- PLACE EVERY CHARACTER IN THE 3D WORLD, not per camera: each character (and animal, key prop) has its position and facing in the world for each moment of the story (from the state table); cameras are then put into that world. Never move a stand-in to suit one camera - if a picture needs it elsewhere, the story moment changed, so the world state changes for every camera of that moment.
- CHECK YOUR OWN LAYOUT BEFORE ANY PICTURE (the user, 10-08: a tale with only 2-3 places and many cameras came out with places that changed from shot to shot): for each place render a TOP-DOWN plan with every camera (position + view cone, numbered) and every character / prop for each story moment, and go through the pictures in order: the same place has the same geography (walls, doors, trees, furniture) in every camera; each character stands where the state table says; screen direction holds; a camera sees what its picture needs. Then compare the layout renders of one place side by side. Fix the world, the cameras or the stand-ins (and the reference 3D models) until it holds - only then generate. In the final check compare every picture with the OTHER pictures of the same place (layout and background must agree), not only with its own layout.
- Recurring buildings, rooms, furniture and big props get proper 3D models (shape, doors, windows, openings where the pictures need them), not single boxes; characters may stay simple stand-ins at true size with a facing marker.
- One 3D world per story, in METRES (Blender in the background: blender --background --python <script>): every place built ONCE with a fixed layout (walls, doors, windows, stove, furniture, fences, wells, trees, the ship...) and every prop at true size, one fixed design per object; a simple stand-in (a coloured post / box) for EVERY character at true size - people 1.50-1.85 m by age, animals at their real species size (look it up), story sizes as the story gives them (a tiny boy, a giant). Size vehicles and buildings from the stand-ins (deck, doors, huts fit the people).
- A camera per picture with a full-frame (36x24 mm) focal length (24 wide / 35-50 normal / 85 close); keep the screen direction (180-degree rule); the camera moves only as the story moves.
- A STATE TABLE per picture: who is in view and where, what they hold, story states (what is stuck / wet / broken, how much of something there is, what covers whom), time of day - put it into the layout (positions, colours, props) and into the prompt.
- Render each camera's layout in flat colours at the generation size (2048x1152 for 16:9) + its JSON (what the camera sees: each figure's box and height as a fraction of the frame, the horizon). Check the layouts as a contact sheet (scale, placement, continuity of each place) before any picture is generated.
- Generating FROM the layout - what worked (tale 39 pilot + bake-off 2026-10-08):
  · GPT Image 2.5 (default for the series): image 1 = the layout render, then the shot's references (single-person pictures, not group sheets; at most ~4-5 - more made Replicate fail); the prompt OPENS with "Repaint image 1 as a finished illustration ... keep its framing EXACTLY - do NOT move the camera closer ... every coloured stand-in replaced by the real character at the same spot with the same height in the frame (colour mapping)", then each figure's head-top and feet as % of the frame from the JSON, the horizon %, who stands where, the state; quality medium, a failed check gets one medium retake, then high.
  · Local Qwen-Image-2.1: the layout BLURRED (Gaussian) as the LAST reference + "composition guide only, no posts or boxes", or (better geometry) its Fun ControlNet on blurred depth at 0.75, then a Qwen edit pass with single-person references at the model's native size.
  · Name the story facts the model gets wrong (no ship's wheel on a tiller boat, nobody on the rail, sails black...) in every prompt where they apply.
- Everyone stands / sits ON the ground, floor or furniture with visible contact - nobody floats; every shadow belongs to whoever casts it; characters who talk turn to and look at each other, not at the camera, unless the story says so.
- CHECK each picture against its layout side by side (an overlay of the layout's boxes and horizon on the picture): camera, horizon, left-to-right order, SCALE (people vs doors / animals / boat / each other), exact count; the place's geography against the other pictures of the same place. Models still draw people ~1.1-1.3x bigger than the layout in close / medium shots - measure it and retake when a figure is clearly off.

- Detail a model didn't draw can't be added afterwards: give what matters (faces, hands, text) enough pixels at generation time, when the model can render that size. Render at least 1280×720 (16:9; as many pixels for other shapes) before any upscaling. An upscaler invents detail and can damage it (faces turn plastic, eyes glassy) - compare it with the original before keeping it.
- Prompts: who (from which reference picture), what they wear or hold (with the details you saw in the references), pose, framing, background, light, style. In English unless the model's card shows otherwise.
- References: one approved picture per character (face, costume), per place and per recurring animal or prop (a rider's horse: coat, mane, saddle), reused everywhere - every picture gets the references of everyone and everything recurring in it. Every recurring character gets a turnaround: front, side and back as SEPARATE pictures (a model given one picture with several figures copies the grid). Make the front first, then the side and back from it (an edit of the front), so the costume stays the same; the back view shows what is NOT on the back (e.g. a cherkesska: plain back, gazyrs only in the two chest panels). Approve each view like a final picture, with the costume rules written out. Give each shot the views that match how the person is seen there (front + back for a figure seen from behind), not every view every time. There is no fixed number of references per picture (the user, 10-07: no cap - the brain decides) - add every reference that helps (each recurring person and animal in it, the view matching the pose, the place, a key prop), and judge by the results, not by a habit of 1-3. Keep reference pictures free of ambiguous details a model may copy wrongly (belt pendants that look like cartridges). If references make renders too slow, measure fewer or smaller ones; never drop them altogether without the user's OK - that changes the method.
- Every character is a different person: different faces, builds, ages, hair, clothes - also in a crowd and among siblings. Twins too get a distinct mark (a scar, an ornament, a colour of their clothes). Check that no two characters came out looking the same.
- Keep sizes true: everything in its real proportion to everything else - people, animals, creatures, buildings, trees, weapons, tools, food, furniture (e.g. a horse is bigger than its rider unless it is a foal, a door fits a person, a cup fits a hand, a giant is drawn as big as the story says). Check scale on every picture, not only faces.
- The culture of the story sets the look: clothes, weapons, buildings, landscape, household things as in the people and place the story comes from (e.g. a Japanese tale: kimono and straw hats, wooden houses, pine-covered mountains) - creatures and buildings too: the story's own (its dragon, giant, fortress as that culture tells them), not generic European fantasy (no white fairy-tale castles or Western dragons unless the story says so). When the references don't show enough, search for real examples (museum pieces, ethnographic photographs) and use them as references. Check garments detail by detail against those references (cut, closures, belts, cartridge pockets, headwear) and retake pictures where they drift.
- Describe each person once in a prompt (a person described twice tends to appear twice), state the exact number of people, and count them in the result - the same for counts the story gives (a nine-headed giant has nine heads). Crowds of five or more are hard for smaller models: keep those layouts simple. When a model ties traits together (e.g. white hair → old age) and the story wants them apart, say the combination outright and check it.
- Change one thing per edit (a hat, a background): every edit can drift the face - check the likeness after each.
- Judge every picture ALONE - never several pictures on one composite sheet (a tale judged 4 pictures per sheet passed an old man with three arms). Judge SHAPES, not only counts: follow each limb from shoulder / hip to its end and read each figure's silhouette - a tail, hair, hat, sleeve or object that could read as an extra head, arm, leg or hand is a FAIL even when the count is right (a capuchin's curled black tail passed two judges as "1 head, 1 tail" and read as a second head); ask the judges that as its own question. A judge's body remark ("hands swapped", an odd joint) is never minor - look at the crop and retake.
- Check every picture on its own at full size, then zoomed crops covering every figure: count heads, arms, hands and legs (animals too), people against the prompt, anyone appearing twice; compare each character (face, hat, coat, their horse's colour) with its reference side by side. Never approve from a contact sheet - sheets are only for comparing continuity across pictures.
- Body counts follow the story: every person's and animal's heads, eyes, ears, arms, hands, fingers, legs, feet, horns, tails and wings as the story gives them (a nine-headed giant, a one-eyed giant, a three-legged horse); where the story says nothing, the normal number for that kind of being. Count on enlarged crops (each half of the picture and each figure) - a full-frame look misses a third ear or two hands on one side - and ask the judge a separate counting-only question; confirm its left / right calls on the crop yourself. A right count can still be a wrong body: hands read as real hands (five digits showing = one of them is a thumb - shorter, set lower on the side, opposing - on the correct side; a thumb may be hidden only where the pose explains it - a fist, a grip, a hand seen from the back or edge-on - and then at most four fingers show; five equal fingers in a row = a fail), each ear / horn / limb grows from the right place, and hair, manes, forelocks and tufts must not read as an extra ear or horn - judge the SHAPE on the crop, not only the number. HANDS need their own pass (two judges once passed a "three" gesture with six digits): crop each visible hand alone at 600 px or more and have it ENUMERATED digit by digit - thumb, index, middle, ring, little, folded or hidden ones included, each with where its tip or nail is - before any total; exactly 5 (or fewer hidden believably), one thumb, nothing doubled. Prefer simple hand poses; counting gestures with folded fingers fail most often.
- A second judge for every picture: another model (preferably Gemini Flash - another family - or Sonnet) gets the picture at full size, its references and a fixed checklist (the counts and comparisons above), and answers in a fixed form. A picture is approved only when you and the judge both pass it; when you disagree, look again. A judge may not know a culture's details (gazyrs, papakha): describe them instead of naming them ("capped cartridge tubes"), give a real reference photo, and ask yes / no per place ("any tubes on the back? on the shoulders? on the belt?"); your own crops of each torso stay the main check for such details.
- Try the local models first; go to a paid one when they fail or the job is clearly beyond them - say why and the cost.
